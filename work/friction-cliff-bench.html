Can reinforcement learning beat model predictive control when a car hits ice?

I don't know yet, and that is deliberate. The simulator, the labels and the plan are done and the comparison is running. I wrote down what I expect and what would prove me wrong before looking at any result.

Everything: design, simulator, controllers, evaluation

Stack

The problem

A car is in a corner on dry road and the surface turns to ice. Grip drops to about a ninth of what it was, in a few metres. Two families of controller claim to handle this:

  • Reinforcement learning: a network trained on millions of simulated skids, which works out the grip from how the car has just been behaving.
  • Model predictive control (here MPPI): simulate thousands of possible futures every instant, pick the best, and update the grip estimate on the fly.

Papers usually report one beating the other. Two things make those comparisons hard to trust. Many test cases are crashes that no controller could avoid, which only adds noise. And the method the authors care about usually gets more tuning.

What I built

First, find out what is possible

Before comparing anything, every scenario gets one of four labels: impossible, possible only if you see the ice coming, possible by reacting, or undetermined. The "impossible" label comes from a proof (the tyres can't supply the force the corner needs, even used perfectly). The "possible" labels come from oracle controllers that are told the true grip.

A fast simulator I can trust

The vehicle model is a bicycle model with Pacejka tyre curves, the standard description of how tyre force saturates and falls off when sliding. I wrote it twice: a slow, readable NumPy version and a GPU version in NVIDIA Warp. Automated tests check that they agree (190+ across the project, pytest and GoogleTest). The GPU version runs about 270 million control steps per second on a laptop GPU. I also wrote the MPPI controller as a C++17/CUDA library: for one car it plans over 1,024 sampled trajectories, 150 steps each, in about 15 ms, inside the 20 ms control step, and runs up to 1.35× faster than the Warp version.

A fair fight, agreed in advance

  • Both methods get the same sensors and the same number of tuning trials, logged in a ledger.
  • The hypotheses, the metrics, and the results that would disprove each hypothesis were committed to the repository before any comparison ran (pre-registration).
  • My prediction, on record: "RL wins" will not survive a properly tuned adaptive baseline.

What I've learned so far

  • Writing down what would prove me wrong changed the design. Several scenarios I had planned turned out to be impossible for any controller.
  • A second, slow implementation is the cheapest test there is for a fast one.
  • Reinforcement learning needed a warm start from a simple controller and about 200 million steps before it was competitive. Stopping earlier would have "shown" that it loses.
