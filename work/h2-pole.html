Can a humanoid balance a free pole on its palm while it walks, using only its head camera?

Yes, in simulation: the pole stays up in 99.0% of walking episodes and 98.8% of standing ones. Getting there took a classical balance controller, a learned estimator, and a lot of tests designed to catch me fooling myself. The real robot is next.

My role
Research assistant. I designed, trained and evaluated the controller.
When
May 2026 – now
Stack
Isaac Lab, PyTorch, PPO, MuJoCo, Unitree DDS
Status
Works in two simulators. Hardware tests planned this winter.

The problem

Balancing a broomstick on your palm is easy to describe and hard to do. The stick is always falling, and the only fix is to keep moving your hand under it. Now do it while walking, when every step shakes the hand.

For the robot there are three more constraints:

  • The pole is free. It is 1.5 m long and stands on an open palm. Nothing clamps it.
  • The robot only sees it through its head camera. There is no sensor on the pole. The camera is noisy, slow, and sometimes wrong.
  • One controller moves all 31 joints. The legs have to walk without shaking the arm more than the arm can correct.

It's a good research task because it can't be faked. A robot that carries a box can be stiff and slow. A pole falls in under a second if the estimate or the reaction is off.

What I built

Pure reinforcement learning didn't work

The first plan was the usual one: reward the robot for keeping the pole up and let it learn everything. It learned to stand with the pole. It never learned to walk with it: 0.2% of walking episodes succeeded.

A classical controller underneath, learning on top

Pole balancing is a textbook control problem with a known solution (LQR). So I put that solution inside the arm, with inverse kinematics to turn "move the palm here" into joint angles. The learned policy then only has to add corrections for what the simple model gets wrong, and make the legs cooperate. This is called residual reinforcement learning.

With that change, the first run went from 0.2% to 75.7% success while walking. Everything after that was about making the numbers honest and then making them high.

A learned estimator

The balance controller needs to know how fast the pole is tipping, and a camera can't measure that directly. A Kalman filter estimates it from a simple model. I trained a small network to do the same job by imitating a "teacher" that sees the true pole state in simulation. The method is DAgger: the student drives, the teacher labels what it should have said, and the student retrains on its own mistakes. The result is about 30% more accurate than the Kalman filter.

The robot's real software interface, before the real robot

Training happens in Isaac Lab. Before asking for robot time, I ran the exported policy in a different simulator (MuJoCo), talking to it through the same messaging layer the real H2 uses (DDS). A policy that only works in the simulator it was trained in has learned that simulator.

What went wrong

These cost the most time and taught me the most.

The estimator that looked twice as good as it was

On recorded data, my learned estimator beat the Kalman filter by a factor of two. In the loop it was much less impressive. The cause was a leak: one input was the palm's velocity, and during data collection the arm had been driven by the true pole state. So the palm's motion gave away the answer. I re-collected the data with the arm driven by the estimate, which is what happens in real use. The honest gain is about 30%.

A better camera made the robot drop the pole

In the second simulator the policy failed within two seconds, but only when the camera was less noisy than in training. The filter adapts to how noisy the camera looks. With clean frames it trusted them fully and passed small contact vibrations straight into the "how fast is it tipping" signal. The fix was one line: never let the filter believe the camera is better than a set floor. Standing through the full camera pipeline went from 0 of 3 to 26 of 30.

A camera model that was too kind

An early version of the simulated camera reported the pole's tipping speed directly. No real camera does that. Results dropped a lot when I replaced it with an honest model, and that drop was the real starting point.

A teacher the student couldn't copy

I tried training a "teacher" policy with perfect information and then a "student" to imitate it from camera data. It failed: the teacher's actions depended on things the student's sensors can't see, so there was nothing consistent to imitate. Only teach from a teacher the student could, in principle, become.

What I'd tell someone starting this: training curves aren't results. Every claim here comes from held-out test runs, and several ideas that looked good in training lost to their own starting point when tested the same way.

What's next
